Review & improvement plan

Hao Cao group site

Component-by-component test of the current build, followed by a prioritised list of changes for each page.

Tested against the live homepage build and the shared stylesheet and scripts (main.css, 5,814 lines · main.js) at 1280 × 540, and against the 980 / 736 px breakpoint rules.

What the site has to do

Five audiences were named for this site, and they arrive wanting different things. Every recommendation below is judged against whether it helps one of them finish their errand.

Grad students & postdocs
Is he taking students, what would I work on, who else is in the group, where did they go afterwards, and how do I make contact?
Peer researchers
Recent papers with resolvable links, which missions and datasets, and what the group can model or measure that mine cannot.
Funders & review panels
Track record, current support, instrument deliverables, students trained — and a CV they can download as a PDF.
Public & press
One sentence they can quote, one image they can use, and a named contact.
Department & admin
Correct title, office, phone, courses taught, and service — all current.

The headline finding. Nothing is broken in a way a visitor would call broken — the hero, the scroll scrub, the mobile menu and every link behave. The real costs are elsewhere: the homepage ships 12.7 MB to say four sentences, and it offers a visitor exactly one destination.

Fixes that apply to every page

These live in the shared head, stylesheet and scripts, so each one is a single edit that improves all seven pages.

P0
Stop shipping the hero video eagerly
Switch to preload="metadata", re-encode at 1280 px wide, and add a WebM source. A clip this short should land near 1.5–2 MB. The poster already carries the first frame, so nothing is lost visually while it streams. This one change removes about 85% of the homepage’s bytes.
P2
Delete the orphaned header image
The 2.6 MB header.jpg is fully overridden by the subpage gradient rule and never reaches a browser. It is repository clutter rather than a performance problem — delete the file and the stale rule that references it.
P0
Delete the unused SVG hero, or bring it back
Roughly 190 lines of markup, 300 lines of field-line maths and a 1 MB PNG are being downloaded and executed for something no visitor sees. Either remove all three, or keep the SVG as the reduced-motion and slow-connection fallback and load the PNG only when it is actually shown. Worth deciding deliberately — it is a lovely piece of work to have hidden.
P1
Make the nav a real target
Give the anchors their own padding and a normal line-height instead of leaning on the list’s zero line-height, so each item is at least 44 px tall. Add aria-current="page" to the active item — the highlight is currently visual only.
P1
Add share and search metadata
Per page: a canonical URL, a distinct title and description, Open Graph and Twitter tags with the Jupiter poster as the share image, a favicon, and one JSON-LD Person block naming the affiliation and ORCID. For a site whose traffic arrives via pasted links and search, this is the cheapest reach available.
P1
Accessibility pass
Remove user-scalable=no; add lang="en" to the html element and lang="zh" on 曹浩; add a skip-to-content link; lift the footer copyright to at least 4.5:1; and promote the research rows’ focus ring to a site-wide default so keyboard users see the same indicator everywhere.
P1
Make the email a link
The address is written as hcao [at] epss.ucla.edu with no mailto anywhere on the site. Spam obfuscation stopped being effective years ago, and it costs a real click for every student and journalist. A plain mailto in the footer and on Opportunities — with a subject line pre-filled on the latter — is the single easiest conversion win here.
P2
Subset the fonts, add a real footer, stamp a date
Load two Source Sans weights instead of six and subset Font Awesome to the glyphs in use. Repeat the nav in the footer so visitors reaching the bottom of a long page have somewhere to go, and add a “last updated” date — on an academic site, freshness is a credibility signal.
P2
Consolidate the stylesheet
Flatten the four stacked refinement passes into one, delete the rules whose markup is gone, and drop the !important chains they created. Do this before any redesign, not after — otherwise every future change needs a fifth override layer.

Home

The one page every audience sees. It currently establishes who he is beautifully, and then offers one door.

  • Give the page more than one exit. Six of the seven content links point at research.html — the five area rows and the button. Publications, Team and Opportunities are reachable only from the top nav. The stylesheet already contains a homepage button row that the markup dropped; bring it back as three primary links: Research, Publications, and Join the group.
  • Say whether you are recruiting, above the fold of the second screen. This is the first question a prospective student has and the site never answers it on the homepage. One line with a date — “Recruiting one PhD student for Fall 2027” — outperforms every other change on this list for that audience.
  • Add a short news list. Four to six dated one-liners: papers out, missions, students graduating, instruments delivered. It answers “is this group active?” for peers and panels at a glance, and it is the section that makes the site worth revisiting.
  • Name the missions in the research rows. “Planetary Interiors” and “Ocean Worlds” are categories, not evidence. Juno, Cassini, Europa Clipper, lunar surface magnetometry — whichever are accurate — tell a reviewer in three words what the abstract takes a paragraph to convey.
  • Break up the intro paragraph. Seventy-one words centred at 1.5em is a wall, and on phones the stylesheet squeezes it into a 17.5 rem column. Lead with the bolded sentence as a standalone line, then set the remainder smaller and left-aligned like every other block on the page.
  • Give the banner a heading. It is currently an orphan paragraph between the H1 and “Research Areas” — the stylesheet still has styles for the heading that was removed. Screen-reader users navigating by heading skip straight past the site’s thesis statement.
  • Top-align the About copy. It is pushed down with an auto margin, so at wide widths the paragraph drifts away from its own heading and away from the portrait.
  • Consider the group photo here. A lab site that shows only one face reads as one person. The team photo below the About block would do more recruiting work than any copy change.

Research

The destination of nearly every homepage link, so it carries most of the site’s explanatory load.

  • One figure per research area. This is the most visual science on the site and the page has no figure styles at all in the stylesheet — strong evidence it is running as unillustrated prose. A field-line map, a gravity profile, an induction response: one image with a real caption per area, credited to the paper it came from.
  • Two registers per area, not one. A plain-language sentence a journalist or first-year can use, then the technical detail. Right now every audience gets the same middle register.
  • Link each area to its papers. A “key papers” line under each section, deep-linked into the anchor on Publications, closes the loop peers came for.
  • Name missions, instruments and funders explicitly. Reviewers and panels scan for these. A short “support” line per area does more than a paragraph of description.
  • End each area with a way in. “Interested in this? Here is what a first project looks like” plus a contact link, aimed straight at Opportunities.

Publications

Built as year-grouped entries with authors, title and journal details. Structurally sound; the gains are in navigation and signalling.

  • A selected-publications tier at the top. Five or six papers with a line each on why they matter. A reverse-chronological list asks the reader to already know which papers count; most visitors do not.
  • A DOI on every entry. Verify no entry is a dead end — this is the one page where a broken link costs real credibility with peers.
  • A sticky year rail. Long lists need a jump-to-year strip; the year headings are already there to anchor to.
  • Mark up authorship. Bold his own name, and mark student and postdoc first authors with a footnoted symbol. For a prospective student, a page full of student first authors is the most persuasive thing on the site — and it costs one legend line.
  • Export links at the top. Google Scholar, ORCID, ADS and a BibTeX file. Peers and admin both arrive wanting to leave with a citation.

Team

A group photo above rosters grouped by role. The most under-used page relative to its recruiting value.

  • People, not a list of names. A card each: photo, role, one line on what they work on, and a link out to their site or Scholar. Applicants are trying to picture themselves in the group; names alone don’t let them.
  • Alumni with placements. Where former students and postdocs went next is the strongest evidence a group can publish, and it is also what panels look for under training record. If the page has no alumni section, this is the highest-value addition on the whole site.
  • Show openings inline. An “open position” card in the roster, linking to Opportunities, catches the visitor at the moment they are already imagining joining.
  • Caption the group photo with names and a date. It is currently decoration; with names it becomes a record.

Opportunities

An intro paragraph plus a contact card with a button. This page has one job and should be judged only on whether people finish it.

  • State status, per role, with dates. PhD, postdoc, undergraduate and visiting, each marked open or closed with the cycle and deadline. Ambiguity here reads as “not really hiring” and costs applications silently.
  • Say what to send. A four-item list — CV, a paragraph on interests, transcript, references — plus a mailto with the subject pre-filled. Removing the guesswork is what raises application quality.
  • Name the funding route and the degree programme. International applicants in particular need to know which UCLA programme to apply through and whether support is available; missing this is the most common reason a good applicant self-eliminates.
  • Add two or three example first projects. Concrete beats general: it lets a candidate write a specific letter, which is easier for them and more useful to you.
  • Stamp the page with a review date so nobody wonders whether the posting is from two cycles ago.

Teaching

Sectioned lists. Read mainly by enrolled students and by department admin, with occasional weight on a tenure or award file.

  • Course number, title, term and level on one line — the four things a student checks before clicking anything.
  • Separate current from past. “Teaching this term” first, then an archive. A single undifferentiated list makes every entry look equally live.
  • One line on what each course covers, plus a syllabus link where one can be public. Students shopping courses are the primary audience.
  • Add mentoring and outreach. Undergraduate research supervision and public talks belong here rather than buried in the CV; both count toward the training record panels ask about.

CV

A clean two-column layout of labelled sections. Its audience — panels and admin — usually wants a file, not a page.

  • A PDF download at the top, with its date in the link text. Reviewers attach files to their notes; a web page cannot be attached. This is the single most requested thing on any academic CV page.
  • A print stylesheet. Someone will print this page. Drop the header band and nav, set type in points, and keep sections from breaking mid-entry.
  • Lead with funding and honours. Grants, awards and service are what a panel reads first; education and employment can follow.
  • Cross-link rather than duplicate. Point the publications section at the Publications page instead of maintaining two lists that will diverge.

Suggested order

1
Weight and metadata — the video, the hidden SVG and its 1 MB PNG, the head tags. Roughly a day, no design decisions, and it makes the homepage usable on a phone on cellular data.
2
Access and contact — nav targets, pinch zoom, focus rings, contrast, a real mailto. Small, mechanical, and it removes every excuse a visitor has to give up.
3
Homepage routing — primary links, a recruiting line, a news list. This is where the site starts converting rather than just informing.
4
Content depth per page — Opportunities and Team first, since they carry the recruiting goal; then Research figures; then Publications tiering and the CV PDF.
5
Stylesheet consolidation — before any visual redesign, so the next change does not need a fifth override layer.

Open questions I would want answered before implementing: is the group currently recruiting, and for which roles? Which missions and instruments can be named publicly? Is there an alumni list with placements? And is there a current CV PDF to link?
